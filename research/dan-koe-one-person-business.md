# Part I — Who Dan Koe Is

## The one-line version

Dan Koe is a writer and digital-product seller who turned "I write for two hours a day and sell what I know" into a business reportedly doing **$3.3M in 2023** and **$4M+ in 2024 at ~98% margins with no full-time employees**. He is the person who most clearly articulated the *one-person business* as a destination rather than a stepping stone to a real company.

## Why the biography matters more than the framework

The single most useful thing about Koe is not his framework — it's his failure log. The framework reads like it was designed. The biography shows it was **reverse-engineered from six years of flailing**. If you only absorb the framework you will badly underestimate the timeline.

### The actual timeline

**College (Arizona State), roughly ages 18–22**

- Switched majors repeatedly. Started a fitness YouTube channel with a dormmate, got ~20K views on one video, abandoned it.
- Arrested for marijuana possession; faced a felony or an expensive diversion program. Bought *The Power of Now* during the fallout. He marks this as a turning point.
- Picked up photography → fell in love with Photoshop → built ~2,500 Instagram followers making surreal composite art.
- Tried a Facebook Ads agency: sent 50 cold emails, landed **zero** clients, quit.
- Tried a rave-clothing dropshipping store: **one sale, $0 net profit**.
- Senior year: took a web development course, "fell in love with coding," skipped 95% of classes and taught himself the curriculum in a month.
- Freelance web design: earned **~$500 total**.
- Started a blue-light-glasses e-commerce brand with **$5,000–$10,000 borrowed from his father**. Spent it on product photography and influencer promos. Lost it. He describes this as his low point.

**Post-college, ages ~22–26**

- Took a web design job to stabilize.
- Tried a second e-commerce brand (minimalist wallets) — no sales. Tried an SEO agency and a content marketing agency simultaneously.
- Freelancing finally worked: 2–3 clients/month at **$1,500–$2,500** for web design.
- Learned email marketing and copywriting *through client work*, then repositioned from "websites" to **"service funnels"** (landing page + opt-in + email sequence) at **$2,500–$5,000**. This repositioning is the first real leverage move in the whole story.
- Quit the job. Found Twitter. Narrowed his audience to creators, coaches, and freelancers.
- **Hit six figures freelancing — four to five years in.**

**The product era**

- Made a freelancing course with **500 Twitter followers**. Networked into bigger accounts' audiences. It added **~$3K/month**.
- Second product: web design fundamentals. Third product at 10K followers: social media.
- Bundled everything into **Modern Money**, which became **Modern Mastery** (a membership, built over ~2 years).
- Pivoted his own client work from *doing marketing* to *consulting on marketing* — teaching instead of executing. Roughly **5 clients at $8K each + membership ≈ $50K/month.**
- Built **Digital Economics** (high-ticket masterclass) and **The 2 Hour Writer** (low-ticket, to feed the ladder).

**The scale era**

- **2022: ~$800K/yr**, writing 2 hours a day.
- **2023: ~$3.3M/yr**, same 2 hours a day. Difference: audience size and leverage, not effort.
- Launched **Kortex**, a note-taking/writing SaaS for creators — reinvesting product profits into software.
- **2024: $4M+ at ~98% margins, no full-time employees.**
- One reported **$139,000 weekend** (Black Friday).

### The three numbers that should reframe everything

| What | Number |
|---|---|
| Years of failed ventures before anything worked | ~4–6 |
| Followers when he sold his first digital product | **500** |
| Increase in effort between $800K/yr and $3.3M/yr | **Zero** — same 2 hrs/day |

The last row is the actual thesis of his entire body of work. Income decoupled from hours. The first row is the part his marketing quietly omits.

# Part II — The Worldview Underneath the Business Model

You can't cleanly separate Koe's business advice from his philosophy, and trying to do so is how people end up executing the tactics badly. The philosophy is load-bearing.

## 1. The "Creator Society"

His claim: information has spread beyond measure, so the institutions that used to gatekeep — schools, churches, employers — are being replaced by internet-native alternatives. The consequences he draws:

- Learning is driven by **curiosity**, not curriculum.
- Goals are **self-generated**, not assigned.
- Advancement happens in **"creator actualization hierarchies"** — you rise by educating and creating value, and nobody can block you.
- Entrepreneurship is the logical endpoint, not a risky detour.

Whether or not you buy the sweep of this, it does describe a real shift: distribution used to be the scarce resource and now attention is.

## 2. The Four Eternal Markets

Everything monetizable, in his framing, sits in one of four buckets:

1. **Health**
2. **Wealth**
3. **Relationships**
4. **Happiness**

The point isn't that these are the only businesses. It's a filter: *are you solving a real, permanent human problem, or an imaginary one?* If your idea doesn't ladder up to one of these, he'd say you have a hobby.

## 3. Anti-Vision before Vision

This is one of his genuinely good ideas and it is free.

> "It's easier to know what you don't want (from experience) than what you want (from imagination)."

**The exercise:**

1. **Observe society.** Look at people around you, their habits, their trajectories. Note the patterns you refuse.
2. **Reflect on your lows.** Write down experiences you never want to repeat — and the root cause of each.
3. **List specifics.** Get uncomfortable. Vague anti-visions don't propel.
4. **Project forward.** Where do your *current* habits put you in 10 years? Is that acceptable?
5. **Transmute.** Now write the exact opposite. That's your vision.

> "Anti-vision is the future you refuse to accept. The dumb mind. The robotic work. The soft body. The numb relationships. Let a terrible image build up in your mind. Remind yourself of it every day. Then channel everything you have into the complete opposite."

The mechanism: **anti-vision is propulsion (pushes you), vision is magnetism (pulls you).** Most goal-setting advice only gives you the second one, which is why it evaporates in February.

## 4. Enjoyment vs. Pleasure ("Dopamine Mastery")

- **Pleasure** — outcome-focused, short-term, requires no effort, normalizes fast ("the honeymoon phase").
- **Enjoyment** — process-focused, investment-based, tracks progress toward something long-term.

His three ways to stay on the enjoyment side:
1. **Mindfulness and detail** — notice texture and nuance in ordinary experience.
2. **Intrinsic philosophy** — find the deeper why beneath the material goal (his example: bodybuilding as therapy).
3. **Learning tied to identity** — build meaning *underneath* the material pursuit.

Relevance to business: a one-person business has no boss, no coworkers, and no external structure. Enjoyment of the *process* is the only thing that survives month eight.

## 5. Focus as a lens, and the "infinite game"

From *The Art of Focus*:

- **Focus is a lens, not a willpower problem.** Once you have a clear goal, you automatically start filtering information through it. Ideas that were invisible become obvious. The goal creates the filter; the filter creates the insight.
- **Identity is constructed** from beliefs, perspectives, language, and assumptions — and it's transient. You can rewrite it deliberately.
- **The infinite game** — the point isn't to win, it's to keep evolving. Businesses built to "exit" and businesses built to "continue" are structurally different.
- **The New Rich** — people who use creativity to make enough money for freedom, without needing a job or a degree. (Direct descendant of Tim Ferriss's *4-Hour Workweek*.)
- **Psychic entropy** — the mental chaos of unstructured attention. Clarity is the antidote.
- **Signal-to-noise ratio** — mental clarity comes from ruthlessly narrowing what you let in. "If you aren't where you want to be in life, nothing is as important as your goals."

## 6. Self-actualization → Self-monetization → Self-transcendence

His compressed arc for the whole thing:

1. **Self-actualization** — improve yourself, solve your own problems.
2. **Self-monetization** — document and teach the journey.
3. **Self-transcendence** — help others get there faster than you did.

# Part III — The Core Model

## The thesis in one paragraph

> "Solve your problems. Change your own life. Package up the method. Slap a price tag on it."

You are not building a company that sells a thing. You are **turning yourself into the business**. Your interests, your problems, and your specific path through them are the product. He argues this is the right model for **95% of people**, especially anyone starting from zero, because it requires no capital, no team, no inventory, and no permission — only an idea and yourself.

## The Experience Model (his inversion of the customer avatar)

Traditional marketing: invent an external customer avatar, research their pain, build for them.

Koe's inversion: **you are the customer avatar.**

1. Solve your own problem.
2. You naturally attract people in a similar position.
3. Systematize what you did.
4. Sell it to people **one level below where you are now** — not to experts, not to the general public.

This solves the beginner's paralysis. You don't need to be an authority; you need to be *further along than the person you're helping.* As he puts it: **"Your imposter syndrome is self-deception."** If you've ever helped a friend or family member with something you learned, you have enough to start.

His metaphor for a "system": *climbing the stairs to the first floor, then sending the elevator down.*

## Three entry paths

**Path 1 — Learn a skill, sell a skill.**
Pick a modern, in-demand skill (email marketing, funnel design, content writing, video editing, automation). Make content demonstrating it. Charge $500–$1,000 for freelance/consulting work.
*Limitation he names:* one-dimensional brand, and client dependency that turns into another job.

**Path 2 — Become a value creator around your interests.**
Invest energy into your own goals across the four eternal markets. Learn the sub-topics. Document it. Become a "modern Renaissance man" — a synthesizer of multiple domains.
*Limitation:* slower to first dollar. Much harder to monetize early.

**Path 3 — Both.** His actual recommendation.
Use the skill to get paid now. Use the interests to build the brand that eventually makes the skill unnecessary. Build landing pages, funnels, emails, and content *for yourself* while charging others for the same work.

He cites Heinlein — *"Specialization is for insects"* — to argue that the multi-dimensional operator who can hire specialists beats the single-skill technician.

## The Four Pillars

### Pillar 1 — Brand: *You are the niche*

> "Your personal brand is your 'self' or 'identity' in the new society."

The chain he describes: **Identity → shapes Perspective → contains Goals → which act as a lens on all information → producing a Vision** that shows which skills and interests you're developing.

Crucially: **start from a goal, not a problem.** Traditional advice says find a problem in the market. He says find where *you're* going, and the problems will surface on the way.

Bio template he gives: **"I write about [skills/topics] so you can [outcome]."**

**The "Niche of One."** His most defensible idea:

> "The most profitable niche is you. Your interests, your problems, and your path to solving them is the unique value proposition that cannot be replicated."

> "If you pick a topic like 'Email Marketing' you are a commodity. If you pick a mission, you are a category of one."

And: **"You don't need a niche, you need a point of view."**

The practical corollary — target **people who are like you, or who want to become like you**, because they share your ideal future. Notably, he spent ~80% of his content on things *outside* his core service (fitness, emotional management, philosophy), which is what pulled in multifaceted people rather than commodity buyers.

**"Mental monopoly"** — occupying irreplaceable real estate in someone's head through genuine curiosity and a story only you can tell.

### Pillar 2 — Content: *Documenting your mind*

Social media as **"a public note-taking system."** Three things you're publishing:

1. What you're learning and how you're applying it
2. Your thoughts and opinions on your skills and interests
3. Life lessons

Key timing claim: **your brand is formed through 1–3 months of content.** It doesn't click from one post. The same message, repeated in different shapes, until the through-line lands.

**The "Domain of Mastery"** — pick ~3 broad topics, then subdivide each into:
- Principles
- Mentors who inspire you in that area
- Sub-topics

That grid is your infinite content calendar. Write at a beginner-accessible level for reach.

**Intelligent imitation** — study the creators and authors who moved you. Document their tone, voice, structure, what they sell, how they sell it. *"You do things 'your own way' by taking the best from people you aspire to be like."* Absorb enough influences and your subconscious recombines them into something original.

**Book-to-brand** — treat your ideal future as the ending of a book. Outline the chapters: what skills and knowledge must be acquired to get there? Those chapters are your content pillars. Then write the story as you live it.

### Pillar 3 — Offer / Product: *Public personal projects*

The progression, which he says takes **1–3 years**:

1. Identify a problem you personally have
2. Build a personal project to solve it (this is where real experience comes from)
3. Turn it into a **Minimum Viable Offer**
4. Freelance or consult at **$500–$1,000**
5. Build an audience with content in parallel
6. Deepen the offer as you learn what clients actually need
7. Productize it so it sells without you

**The Minimum Viable Offer (MVO)** — the most actionable single idea in his whole system. Your first offer is one of:

- A **single-skill freelance service** at $500–$1,000, or
- A **single-interest consulting package** — e.g. 4 calls for $500–$1,000

Why so early, and so small:

> "You NEED something to iterate on. You need something to build, improve, and make more valuable."

> "Your first offer WILL suck."

The MVO is not a revenue strategy. It's a **learning instrument**. It gets you real buyers, real objections, and real feedback, which is the only input that makes version two good. People who wait until the offer is good never ship one.

And: **"Teaching is how you identify knowledge gaps. That is the only way you will be able to fill them."**

### Pillar 4 — Marketing: *Sell to yourself*

Three steps, and they map exactly onto the Experience Model:

**Step 1 — Identify their goal / desired outcome.** Make it top-of-mind. Because you *were* them, you can reflect what's desirable back at them accurately.

**Step 2 — Identify what's in the way.** Diagnostic question: *"What's preventing you from achieving [goal]?"*

**Step 3 — Present your solution to a burning problem.** Qualify whether they're actually a fit. Illustrate what life looks like on the other side. Then make the offer.

The blunt part, which is where most thoughtful people fail:

> "You won't make a single penny if you never promote yourself."
> "If you don't promote your offer, you aren't going to make money."

# Part IV — The Mechanics (how the machine actually runs)

This is the operational layer. Part III is the map; this is the engine.

## The Three-Stage Leverage Roadmap

He frames the common failure as: **99% of creators never build digital leverage** — the ability to reach and monetize far beyond your direct follower count.

### Stage 1 — Low Leverage (0–10K followers)

**Do:**
- Learn what high-performing content actually looks like. Structure: **Hook → Value → Conclusion.**
- Use templates as training wheels. Not forever.
- Prioritize short-form for growth (X posts, reels, TikToks).

**Revenue:** minimal. Accept this.

**The trap:** becoming a brand with high followers and zero loyalty. Reach without trust monetizes at roughly nothing.

### Stage 2 — Medium Leverage (10K–80K followers)

**Do:**
1. **Learn direct response marketing** properly — buyer psychology, conversion.
2. **Build a high-ticket offer** — freelancing, consulting, or coaching at **$2,500–$5,000/month per client**, targeting ~4 clients.
3. **Branch into long-form** — newsletter, podcast, YouTube. Depth, not just reach.

**Benchmark:** roughly **$1 per follower per month** (50K followers ≈ $50K/mo). Treat this as directional, not a law.

**The trap:** you've built yourself a second 9–5. Four high-ticket clients will consume every hour you have, and you'll have no time left to build the thing that replaces them. This is the stage where most people stall permanently.

### Stage 3 — High Leverage (80K+ followers)

**Do:**
1. **Productize and network.** Turn your service system into a product. Build 5–10 genuine peer relationships for cross-pollination.
2. **Diversify content.** Balance short-form (growth) + authority content (results/proof) + authenticity content (long-form synthesis).
3. **Prioritize long-form.** Become a **synthesizer** — connecting multiple disciplines is what makes you un-substitutable.

**What it buys:** the ability to disappear for months. Control income within a range. Choose opportunities rather than accept them. His $139K weekend lives here.

## Distribution: Built, Borrowed, Bought

| Type | What it is | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Built** | Audience you grow with content, then **de-platform** to a newsletter/community you own | Slowest, highest value. The newsletter is the asset. |
| **Borrowed** | Podcasts, guest spots, mentions, collabs | How he sold a course with 500 followers. Most underrated by beginners. |
| **Bought** | Ads, sponsorships | Accelerant only. Never the foundation. |

**De-platforming** — moving people from rented land (X, LinkedIn, IG) to owned land (email) — is non-negotiable in his model. Social gets discovery; email gets revenue.

## The content flywheel

The single highest-leverage operational system he teaches.

**One deep-work session → one cornerstone asset → 20+ derivative pieces.**

```
Choose a high-performing topic
        ↓
Write a LONG newsletter (~2,000–5,000 words)   ← the cornerstone
        ↓
   ├── Newsletter structure  → YouTube video script
   ├── Section headers       → X thread hooks
   ├── "Golden nugget" lines → LinkedIn posts
   └── Individual tweets     → repurposed to LI + IG
```

Cadence: weekly cornerstone. He describes **one hour of deep thinking producing 20 pieces of content.**

Note the direction: **top-down, long-to-short.** Most people do the opposite — grind out daily short posts and never build depth. He writes the deep thing first and mines it. This is why 2 hours/day is sufficient.

## The writing framework: Pain → Insight → Action

1. **Pain (the hook).** Open on a struggle the reader is currently in. *"Do you feel…?"*
2. **Insight (the reframe).** Give them a new lens on the problem. This is what makes it feel valuable rather than obvious.
3. **Action (the utility).** A concrete 3-step system or exercise they can run today.

**Self-test:** label each section of your draft. If one of the three is missing, the piece will underperform. Predictably.

**On hooks:** *"When you choose the right topic, the hook becomes simple to write. If the hook is difficult to write, choose a different topic before you spend a lot of time writing."* This is a very good diagnostic — the hook is a test of the idea, not a copywriting exercise.

Hook criteria: relevance to everyday life, a pain resolved or a benefit promised, and a clear *what's in it for the reader*.

**Key metric he names:** *"Time under attention is the only metric that matters for building deep authority."* Long-form captures hearts; short-form grabs eyes.

## The value ladder

His stated structure, bottom to top:

| Rung | Offer | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Free content / lead magnet | Attention → email |
| 1 | Low-ticket ebook or course | First purchase. Converts a reader into a buyer. |
| 2 | Membership / mid-tier | Recurring revenue, community |
| 3 | High-ticket coaching / consulting / program | Margin and transformation |

His own ladder, made concrete: free newsletter → free/cheap books → **2 Hour Writer ($150)** → **Modern Mastery ($27/mo)** → **Digital Economics ($499–999)** → **Kortex (SaaS)**.

The strategic logic: the low-ticket product exists to convert readers into *buyers*, because the buyer→buyer conversion is enormously easier than reader→buyer. It's not primarily a profit center.

## World-building

The subtlest of his systems. Rather than a funnel, build an **ecosystem people want to live inside**:

- Weave one coherent philosophy through every touchpoint — newsletter, book, community, product
- Build a place where the audience congregates (Modern Mastery)
- Develop signature language and terminology ("anti-vision," "the 2-hour writer," "mental monopoly")
- Result: selling pressure drops, because people buy for **belonging**, not features

This is also why he sells "transformation of identity," not information. Information is free and everyone knows it. Identity isn't.

## The daily operating system

- **Deep work: 2–4 hours/day.** Most days he works 4–5 hours total.
- **The 2-hour morning writing block**, protected, forever. Three stated reasons: it generates base income, it clears his mind, and it cultivates creativity daily.
- **Limit top priorities to 2–3 tasks.** Forces ruthlessness.
- **Reduce friction:** same wake time, same environment, no decisions before the work starts. Creative energy is spent by decisions, not just by working.
- **Build-while-learning:** don't study, then do. Pick a project, work on it daily regardless of competence, hit real problems, and research *only* the specific solution you need. Learning happens through problem-solving; consumption without a project is entertainment.
- **When stuck, zoom out.** Walk, rest, socialize. Let the subconscious work.

**The skill-list exercise:** write 10–20 *specific* skills required by your vision. Not "web design" — *"creating landing pages that communicate business value."* Specificity is what makes the filter work.

# Part V — Honest Critique (what doesn't hold up)

You asked for context to make your own decision, so here is the other side. This is drawn from independent reviews and forum discussion, plus my own reading of the internal logic.

## Fair criticisms

**1. Survivorship, at scale.** He has pulled tens of thousands of people into the creator economy. The overwhelming majority never make meaningful money. His own path took 4–6 years of failure before the first six figures — that part is in his story but not in his marketing.

**2. "Inspirational but not actionable."** The most common substantive complaint. The philosophy is dense and the tactics are thin. You will finish a 4,000-word essay feeling changed and still not know what to post tomorrow. Some of this is deliberate — the tactics are the paid product — but even the paid products are criticized for abstraction.

**3. The model is audience-dependent, and audiences are the bottleneck.** Everything downstream of "build an audience" works. Building the audience from zero is brutally hard, takes far longer than implied, and is the step with the highest failure rate. The framework is strongest exactly where it's least needed.

**4. No refund policy on the membership** — cancellation only. This draws consistent criticism.

**5. The "$1 per follower" benchmark is his math, not a law.** It reflects a highly engaged, self-selected, business-topic audience monetized through a mature ladder. Import it at your own risk.

**6. Circularity.** He teaches building a one-person business by selling a course on building a one-person business, to an audience built by writing about building a one-person business. This doesn't make it wrong — the freelancing and consulting phases were real and generalizable — but the peak of the model is partly self-referential. Ask hard whether the specific engine works in a domain that isn't "teaching the creator economy."

**7. Thin independent verification.** Revenue figures are self-reported. Outside reviews on Trustpilot/Reddit are sparse. Trust scores from review aggregators sit around 4.2/5 — not a scam, but not independently audited either.

## What genuinely holds up

- **The Minimum Viable Offer.** Charge $500 for something small, now, and iterate. This is correct, cheap, and immediately testable.
- **The Experience Model.** Sell to who you were one to two years ago. This is the single best answer to "who am I to teach this."
- **Anti-vision.** Costs nothing, works, and is more durable than goal-setting.
- **The newsletter-first content flywheel.** Mechanically sound regardless of niche. Long-form first, mine downward.
- **Pain → Insight → Action.** A real structural test you can apply to any draft in 30 seconds.
- **De-platforming to email.** Platform-independent, permanently true.
- **Borrowed distribution.** He sold a product with 500 followers by borrowing bigger audiences. Beginners systematically under-use this.
- **The two-hour discipline.** The claim that $800K → $3.3M happened at constant effort is the strongest single argument for leverage over hustle.

## Who this model fits, and who it doesn't

**Good fit if you:**
- Already write, or want to
- Can sustain 1–2 years before it replaces an income
- Have current income and can invest patiently
- Want to build something durable around your own point of view
- Value philosophical grounding alongside tactics

**Bad fit if you:**
- Need income within 3–6 months
- Dislike creating content or being visible
- Want step-by-step tactical instruction
- Want a business that doesn't depend on your personal brand
- Want an asset you can eventually sell (a personal brand has a hard ceiling and is nearly impossible to exit)

**The most important structural caveat:** in this model, **you are the product, so you can never fully leave.** That's a real cost. Koe mitigates it with a SaaS (Kortex) — building something that generates value without his face on it. If your goal is optionality rather than income, note that the personal-brand business is one of the least transferable assets you can build.

# Part VI — Applying It To Yourself

This is where the research stops and the work starts. Do these in order. Write your answers down — the writing *is* the thinking, which is arguably his central claim.

## Step 0 — Decide what you actually want

Before any of the mechanics. The framework optimizes for **time freedom + location freedom + income decoupled from hours**. If what you actually want is a large company, an equity outcome, or deep specialist work inside an organization, this model is not just suboptimal — it's the wrong tool.

> **Prompt:** In one paragraph, what does a good Tuesday look like in three years? Not the revenue. The Tuesday.

## Step 1 — Write your anti-vision

Follow the process in Part II.5. Don't skip to the vision.

> **Prompts:**
> - What are five specific things about how people around you live and work that you refuse to accept for yourself?
> - What were your three lowest points, and what was the actual root cause of each?
> - If you change nothing, where are you in 10 years? Be specific. Job, body, mind, relationships, money, how you spend a Tuesday.
> - Is that acceptable? Where exactly does it become unacceptable?

Then flip every line. That flipped document is your vision.

## Step 2 — Inventory yourself (the raw material)

Four lists. Be exhaustive before you filter.

**A. Skills you can already do at a level others would pay for.** Include things that don't feel impressive to you. Almost everyone undervalues the skills they acquired at work, because competence feels like baseline once you have it.

**B. Problems you have personally solved.** Any domain. Health, money, career, relationships, technical, logistical. For each, note: *how long did it take, and what would you tell someone at the start?* This list is your product catalogue.

**C. Genuine obsessions.** The things you read about with no expectation of payoff. Where do your browser tabs actually go? What do you talk about too long at dinner?

**D. Unfair advantages.** Access, credentials, industry exposure, a network, a specific vantage point most people writing on the internet do not have. Insider knowledge of a real industry is worth more than generalist advice, and is far less contested.

## Step 3 — Find your intersection

The "niche of one" is not one topic. It's **the overlap of three of your topics that no one else combines the same way.**

> **Prompt:** Pick three items across lists A–D. Write the sentence: *"I'm the person who understands [X] and [Y], which is why I see [Z] differently."*
>
> Write five versions. Look for the one that makes you slightly uncomfortable to publish — that's usually the true one, and discomfort is a signal you're saying something not already said.

Then fill his bio template: **"I write about ___ so you can ___."**

## Step 4 — Identify who you were 1–2 years ago

The Experience Model. This is your customer.

> **Prompts:**
> - Who were you 18 months ago? What did you believe that was wrong? What were you stuck on?
> - What do you now know that would have saved that person six months?
> - Where do those people currently hang out, online and offline?
> - What are they *already paying for* that isn't working?

If you can't answer the last one, you're not close enough to a real market yet.

## Step 5 — Design your Minimum Viable Offer

Not a course. Not a book. Not "a platform." One of these two:

- **A single-skill service, $500–$1,000.** One deliverable, one outcome, tightly scoped.
- **A consulting package, $500–$1,000.** Four calls with a defined outcome.

> **Prompts:**
> - What is the single most concrete outcome I can reliably produce for the person in Step 4?
> - Can I describe it in one sentence without the word "help"?
> - What is the smallest version I could deliver **this month**?
> - Who are three specific people I could offer it to by name, this week?

Then apply his marketing frame:
1. What is their desired outcome?
2. What's in the way?
3. Why is my thing the bridge?

Assume version one is bad. That's the design spec, not a failure.

## Step 6 — Build the content engine

**Domain of Mastery.** Pick three topics from Step 3. For each, list: core principles, 3–5 mentors/sources, and 10 sub-topics. That's a grid of ~90 content ideas. You will not run out.

**Cadence.** One long-form cornerstone per week (aim 1,500–3,000 words to start). Mine it downward per the flywheel in Part IV. Do not start with daily short posts — you'll have volume with no depth and nothing to sell.

**Structure.** Every piece: Pain → Insight → Action. Label the sections. Fix what's missing.

**Own the list from day one.** Set up the email list before you have subscribers. Every platform post should have a path to it.

**Borrow distribution deliberately.** Make a list of 20 people one or two tiers above you in your space. Engage genuinely. Podcasts, collaborations, guest posts. He sold his first product to 500 followers by doing this — it is the single fastest lever available to a beginner and nearly everyone ignores it.

## Step 7 — Set the operating rhythm

- Block **2 hours** at the same time daily. Protect it like a client meeting. It is the business.
- Limit yourself to **2–3 priorities** a day.
- Reduce friction: same time, same place, decided the night before.
- **Build while learning.** Don't take a course first. Start the project, hit a wall, research the exact wall, keep going.
- Write the **10–20 specific skills** your vision requires. Not categories — specific capabilities.

## Step 8 — Set honest expectations

Based on his actual timeline, not his marketing:

| Horizon | Realistic target |
|---|---|
| Months 1–3 | Anti-vision + vision written. Niche sentence drafted. 12 cornerstone pieces published. Email list exists. MVO defined. |
| Months 3–6 | **First paid client at $500–$1,000.** Version two of the offer, informed by real feedback. ~1,000 followers, ~200 email subs. |
| Months 6–12 | Offer raised. 2–4 clients. Content engine running without heroics. Clear read on whether your positioning resonates. |
| Year 1–2 | Meaningful side income. First productized offer. The "am I actually going to do this" question is answered by data, not feeling. |
| Year 2–4 | Possible replacement income — *if* the audience compounded. |

**Do not quit your job at month three.** Koe kept a web design job through the failures and only left once freelance income was proven. His own six-figure mark came four to five years in.

## Step 9 — The go/no-go test

After 90 days of running Steps 6–7, answer honestly:

1. Did I actually publish weekly, or did I plan to?
2. Did writing energize or drain me? (The 2 hours/day is only sustainable if it's enjoyment, not pleasure — Part II.4.)
3. Did anyone respond in a way that suggested real value, unprompted?
4. Did I make one offer and ask for money?
5. Would I keep doing this if it never made a dollar?

**If #5 is no, and #1 was "planned to," this is the wrong model for you** — and finding that out in 90 days for the cost of your time is an excellent outcome. Don't buy the $999 program to fix a motivation problem.
